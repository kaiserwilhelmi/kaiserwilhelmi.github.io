Forecast Method Comparison

This page compares forecasting methods on one series at a time. Pick one of nine published test series, one of the /*__COUNT__*/ price, wage, freight and currency series from the Industry Market Watch, or upload your own. It tests up to fourteen methods on the series' own past, recommends the approach that held up best, and gives a range to plan around.

Univariate forecasting. Every forecast here is built from the series' own history and nothing else. No prices, promotions, weather, lead indicators or order books go in. That works when past patterns keep repeating, and it can't see a change coming from something the history has never shown. When it fits and when it doesn't.

One series, its own history

A univariate method learns from a single column of numbers: its level, trend, seasonal pattern and the usual size of its swings. It assumes those carry on. That makes a simple, strong baseline, and on single series it is hard to beat with more complex models (Makridakis, Spiliotis & Assimakopoulos, 2018).

Where it fits. Demand or prices with a steady pattern. A baseline for many items at once, where building a separate driver model for each one isn't practical. Cases where the drivers are unknown, or can't be known far enough ahead to help.

Where it doesn't. Anything the history hasn't seen: a planned promotion or price change, a new customer or contract, a product launch or phase-out, a supplier outage, a tariff. The forecast misses these until they show up in the data, and then it lags behind them. The Victoria electricity series shows the limit: summer heat drives its peaks, and no method here knows the temperature. When a known driver moves the number and you have its value ahead of time, a regression or dynamic regression on that driver usually does better (Hyndman & Athanasopoulos, 2021, ch. 7 and 10). The driver usually has to be forecast as well, though, and errors in that forecast cut into the gain (same, §7.6).

How I'd use it. As the statistical baseline, then adjusted by hand for events I know are coming. In a study of four supply-chain companies, adjustments helped mainly when they were large and based on information the model didn't have, such as a promotion, while small tweaks mostly added error (Fildes, Goodwin, Lawrence & Nikolopoulos, 2009).

How the test works

Rolling origin. At each origin in the backtest window, every method is fitted only on data before that point and forecasts the next h periods, which are then compared with what actually happened (Tashman, 2000; Hyndman & Athanasopoulos, 2021, §5.10). Only origins whose whole h-step path can be checked are scored, so every step ahead counts the same number of times. The window must therefore be at least twice the horizon.

Fitting. Every smoothing method fits its weights and its starting values together by minimising one-step squared error with a Nelder–Mead search. For simple, Holt and damped smoothing the weights may run from 0.0001 to 0.9999 and damping from 0.80 to 0.98, the bounds of the R forecast package (Hyndman & Khandakar, 2008), and the fits match the statsmodels library's. A Holt-Winters fit with restarts matched statsmodels more closely but forecast 1 to 2% worse on the 80 Market Watch series, so the simpler search was kept. The airline model (seasonal ARIMA) is fitted by conditional least squares. Ridge regression works on percentage changes when a series is always positive, and the boosted trees choose how many trees to use on a holdout. Every number was re-computed by an independent Python implementation and matches; accuracy is in line with the statsmodels library's maximum-likelihood fits.

Testing the choice. The top score in any ranking is partly luck, so the method that tops it usually does worse afterwards (Cawley & Talbot, 2010). On the 80 Market Watch monthly series at 6 months ahead, picking the #1 method from earlier data and scoring it on what came next gave 24% more error than the #1's own score in the table. So the page also tests its own advice: at each past origin it ranks the methods on the errors known then, takes the top one and the top three, and scores what they forecast next.

Recommendation. The page recommends the equal-weight average of the three best-ranked methods, because pooling a few top methods is more reliable than betting on one (Kourentzes, Barrow & Petropoulos, 2019) and equal weights are hard to beat (Smith & Wallis, 2009). Tested that way on the 80 Market Watch series with a 24-month window, the pool beat the re-picked single best method at 6 months ahead (50 of 80 series, Wilcoxon p = 0.001) and at 12 months ahead (p = 0.015), and matched it at 1 month. The page switches to the single best method only if that beat the pool by a margin unlikely to be luck, and to naive if the chosen approach did not beat naive. Neither rule changed average accuracy in a test a year back, but naive is the simpler plan when no model earns its place.

Forecast ranges. A range comes from the recommended approach's own misses at each step ahead: the last 48 it made in the backtest, with the pool re-picked at each past origin exactly as it is today. These are empirical prediction intervals (Williams & Goodman, 1971; Lee & Scholtes, 2014). The bounds are the split-conformal order statistics of those misses (Lei, G'Sell, Rinaldo, Tibshirani & Wasserman, 2018), which sit a little outside the plain percentiles so that a range built from a few dozen misses is not too narrow. Nine ways of building the ranges were tested on the 80 Market Watch series over the last five years, each range scored only on what happened after it was built. Ranges from the last 24 misses held only 62 to 73% of the time at a nominal 80%, and ranges from every past miss 67 to 68%: ranges that are too narrow are a long-known problem (Chatfield, 1993), and old misses understate recent volatility (Barber, Candès, Ramdas & Tibshirani, 2023). The last 48 with conformal bounds came closest, holding 77 to 81% of the time at 80% and 87 to 95% at 95%. No rule is right on every series, so the page also shows how often the ranges held on yours.

Significance. The Diebold-Mariano test (1995) uses an equal-weighted cosine variance with fixed-b critical values, which keeps its false-alarm rate near 5% with the short backtests used here (Lazarus, Lewis, Stock & Watson, 2018; Coroneo & Iacone, 2020). The version used before, with the Harvey, Leybourne & Newbold (1997) correction, called a method significantly better up to 15% of the time when nothing differed. A table runs many tests at once, so its p-values are Holm-adjusted (Holm, 1979). "Tied" means a method was not shown to be worse than the #1.

No method is favoured. Every method, including the two fixed combinations, is a candidate. The backtest on your series decides the ranking and which three go into the pool.

Ranking. MASE ranks the methods. Within one series it orders them exactly as MAE does; its scaling lets errors be compared across series (Hyndman & Koehler, 2006). RMSE breaks ties. Demand with many zero periods is the exception (next paragraph).

Demand with many zero periods. Spare parts and MRO items often go months without an order. The page measures this with the average gap between demands (ADI) and how much the order sizes vary (CV²), and calls demand intermittent when the gap is over 1.32 periods, the cut-off of Syntetos, Boylan & Croston (2005). On such a series the average miss (MAE) rewards forecasting zero (Teunter & Duncan, 2009; Kolassa, 2016), while reorder points and budgets need the average rate. So these series are ranked by RMSE, which targets the average, and no forecast goes below zero. Tested on 500 real Walmart item series from the M5 data (Makridakis, Spiliotis & Assimakopoulos, 2022), this cut the pool's error by 5% one day ahead and 2% seven days ahead on daily sales, and by 1 to 2% on weekly sales. I also tested the methods built for this kind of demand: Croston (1972), its bias-corrected version SBA (Syntetos & Boylan, 2005) and TSB (Teunter, Syntetos & Babai, 2011), fitted the way Kourentzes (2014) recommends. They did not help on the daily series and made the weekly 4-week forecast 1.8% worse, so under the rule set before the test they are not among the candidates.

What the metrics mean
  • MAE: average miss in the series' units. Easy to explain to a supplier or a CFO.
  • MSE / RMSE: square the misses first, so one big miss counts heavily. Use when large errors are costly (stockouts, budget overruns). Ranks demand with many zero periods, where it points at the average rate.
  • MAPE: average miss as a % of actual. Intuitive, but undefined at zero, explodes near zero and penalises over-forecasts more than under-forecasts.
  • sMAPE: divides by the average of actual and forecast; bounded at 200%, but still unstable near zero.
  • MASE: MAE divided by the in-sample MAE of a seasonal-naive forecast, which is the typical change over one seasonal cycle. For a trending price that yardstick is large, so a MASE below 1 is easy to reach and is not proof of skill.
  • vs naive, vs seasonal naive: MAE divided by the benchmark's MAE on exactly the same forecasts. −15% means misses 15% smaller than the benchmark's (Davydenko & Fildes, 2013). This is the fair comparison.
  • Bias (ME): average of actual minus forecast. Persistently positive = under-forecasting.

Machine learning vs statistical methods

Before adding the two machine-learning methods (ridge regression and gradient-boosted trees on recent changes), all candidates were backtested on 80 real monthly price, wage, freight and currency series from FRED, plus six series with other time units. Theta, ridge and seasonal ARIMA had the lowest median error (MASE 0.31). But when the method was picked from an older window and scored on the newest 12 months, adding the new methods cut average error only from 0.490 to 0.486, which is not statistically significant (Wilcoxon p = 0.42). A small neural network was also tested and dropped: it ranked worst and was six times slower.

That matches the research. On short single series, statistical methods usually match or beat machine learning (Makridakis, Spiliotis & Assimakopoulos, 2018). Machine learning won the M5 competition because it learned from tens of thousands of related series plus prices and promotions (Makridakis et al., 2022). So the ML methods are here as candidates, and they win only when your data says so: ridge, for example, handles fast exponential growth that the smoothing methods miss.

Data

  • Published test series (nine) are built into the page. Each is a well-known series from forecasting textbooks and studies, picked to cover a different pattern, and each is credited to its original publisher:
    • US retail sales, not seasonally adjusted (Jan 2010 to Sep 2026): U.S. Census Bureau, Advance Monthly Retail Trade Survey, series RSXFSN, retrieved from FRED on 25 Nov 2026. The October 2026 advance estimate is left out because it will be revised.
    • International airline passengers (1949 to 1960): Box, G. E. P., & Jenkins, G. M. (1976). Time Series Analysis: Forecasting and Control, series G. Copy from R's datasets package (AirPassengers).
    • Antidiabetic drug sales, Australia (Jul 1991 to Jun 2008): Australian Pharmaceutical Benefits Scheme, Medicare Australia. Copy from the fpp2 package (a10), Hyndman & Athanasopoulos (2018).
    • Beer production, Australia (1956 Q1 to 2010 Q2): Australian Bureau of Statistics, CC BY 4.0. Copy from fpp2 (ausbeer).
    • US gasoline product supplied (weekly, Feb 1991 to Jan 2017): U.S. Energy Information Administration. Copy from fpp2 (gasoline).
    • Electricity demand, Victoria (daily, 2014): Australian Energy Market Operator, half-hourly demand as distributed in fpp2 (elecdemand), averaged to days by me. AEMO allows use of its material with attribution.
    • One Walmart item, daily unit sales (HOBBIES_1_006, store CA_1, Apr 2012 to May 2016, from its first sale): M5 competition data (Makridakis, Spiliotis & Assimakopoulos, 2022), CC BY 4.0 (Zenodo record). The item was picked by a fixed rule: the first hobbies item with an average gap between sales of 2 to 3 days and more than 1,400 days of history.
    • Canadian dollars per US dollar (Jan 2010 to Oct 2026): Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve System, G.5 release, series EXCAUS, retrieved from FRED on 25 Nov 2026.
    • Nile river flow at Aswan (1871 to 1970): Cobb, G. W. (1978). The problem of the Nile. Biometrika, 65(2), 243–251; used in Durbin, J., & Koopman, S. J. (2012). Time Series Analysis by State Space Methods (2nd ed.). Copy from R's datasets package (Nile).
    I checked every value against a second copy of its source (R's own source files, the fpp2 package files, FRED and the raw M5 file). All match.
  • Market Watch series (/*__COUNT__*/) are loaded when you pick one, from the Industry Market Watch, which refreshes them every day from their original publishers: the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics (producer and consumer prices, hourly earnings), the U.S. Energy Information Administration, the World Bank (commodity prices), the Federal Reserve Board (exchange rates) and the U.S. Bureau of Transportation Statistics (freight). History starts in January 2004, or when the series starts. Series published daily or weekly are monthly averages.
  • Recent spot prices cover about the last year, daily or weekly as published: U.S. Energy Information Administration Open Data, and USDA Agricultural Marketing Service market news.
  • A month or day the publisher skipped (for example October 2025 for some BLS consumer price series) is filled by a straight line between its neighbours, as on Market Watch, and the page says so when it happens. Filled values help fit the methods but are never scored as test targets.
  • Sample demand (units) is synthetic: trend + seasonality + random noise, generated with a fixed seed for demonstration. It is not real company data.
  • Sample spare-part demand is synthetic too: an order in about 4 of every 10 months, sizes of 1 to about 15, with orders thinning out in the last two years. It is not real company data.
  • The tests of the intermittent-demand handling used 500 Walmart item series from the M5 competition (Makridakis, Spiliotis & Assimakopoulos, 2022). Only the one item above is part of this page.
