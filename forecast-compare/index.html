How the test works

Rolling origin. For each of the last N periods, every method is fitted only on data before that point and forecasts the next h periods. Forecasts are compared with what actually happened. This mimics real use and avoids judging a model on data it was fitted to (Tashman, 2000; Hyndman & Athanasopoulos, 2021, §5.10).

Fitting. Holt-Winters fits its three weights and its starting level, trend and seasonal pattern together by minimising one-step squared error (Nelder–Mead search). Seasonal ARIMA is fitted by conditional least squares. The simpler smoothing methods pick their weights from a grid (0.05 to 0.95); full fitting changed their accuracy by under 1% on these series. Every number was re-computed by an independent Python implementation and matches; accuracy is in line with the statsmodels library's maximum-likelihood fits.

Significance. A Diebold-Mariano test (with the Harvey-Leybourne-Newbold small-sample correction) checks whether the winner's lower error could be luck, against both naive and the runner-up. When it could, the page names the simplest method that is not significantly worse.

No method is favoured. The backtest on your series picks the winner. Two combinations (simple averages of other methods) are entered as candidates because averaging often reduces error (Bates & Granger, 1969; Makridakis et al., 2020), but they only win when the data says so. The page also checks whether the same method wins with 12, 24 and 36-period windows.

Ranking. MASE decides the winner because it compares methods on a common, scale-free basis and does not break when values approach zero (Hyndman & Koehler, 2006).

What the metrics mean

  • MAE: average miss in the series' units. Easy to explain to a supplier or a CFO.
  • MSE / RMSE: square the misses first, so one big miss counts heavily. Use when large errors are costly (stockouts, budget overruns).
  • MAPE: average miss as a % of actual. Intuitive, but undefined at zero, explodes near zero and penalises over-forecasts more than under-forecasts.
  • sMAPE: divides by the average of actual and forecast; bounded at 200%, but still unstable near zero.
  • MASE: MAE divided by the in-sample MAE of a seasonal-naive forecast. Below 1 means the method beats that simple benchmark.
  • Bias (ME): average of actual minus forecast. Persistently positive = under-forecasting.

Does machine learning win? An honest test

Before adding the two machine-learning methods (ridge regression and gradient-boosted trees on recent changes), all candidates were backtested on 80 real monthly price, wage, freight and currency series from FRED, plus six series with other time units. Theta, ridge and seasonal ARIMA had the lowest median error (MASE 0.31). But when the method was picked from an older window and scored on the newest 12 months, adding the new methods cut average error only from 0.490 to 0.486, which is not statistically significant (Wilcoxon p = 0.42). A small neural network was also tested and dropped: it ranked worst and was six times slower.

That matches the research. On short single series, statistical methods usually match or beat machine learning (Makridakis, Spiliotis & Assimakopoulos, 2018). Machine learning won the M5 competition because it learned from tens of thousands of related series plus prices and promotions (Makridakis et al., 2022). So the ML methods are here as candidates, and they win only when your data says so: ridge, for example, handles fast exponential growth that the smoothing methods miss.

Data

  • Market Watch series (100) are loaded when you pick one, from the Industry Market Watch, which refreshes them every day. The monthly series come from FRED (Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis) and are credited to their original publishers: the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics (producer and consumer prices, hourly earnings), the International Monetary Fund (commodity prices), the U.S. Energy Information Administration, the Federal Reserve Board (exchange rates) and Cass Information Systems (freight). History starts in January 2004, or when the series starts. Series that FRED publishes daily or weekly are monthly averages.
  • Recent spot prices cover about the last year, daily or weekly as published: U.S. Energy Information Administration Open Data, and USDA Agricultural Marketing Service market news.
  • A month or day the publisher skipped (for example October 2025 for some BLS consumer price series) is filled by a straight line between its neighbours, as on Market Watch, and the page says so when it happens.
  • Sample demand (units) is synthetic: trend + seasonality + random noise, generated with a fixed seed for demonstration. It is not real company data.
