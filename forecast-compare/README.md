# Forecast Method Comparison

**Business question:** Which forecasting method should a buyer trust for a given demand or price series, and how wrong is it likely to be?

It is a univariate tool: every forecast comes from the series' own history, with no outside drivers (prices, promotions, weather, lead indicators). The page says so up front and explains when that fits and when it doesn't (Fildes, Goodwin, Lawrence & Nikolopoulos, 2009, on adjusting a statistical baseline for known events; Hyndman & Athanasopoulos, 2021, ch. 7 and 10, on driver models).

The page backtests up to fourteen methods (statistical and machine learning) on a series with a rolling origin, scores them on seven error measures, and lets the data pick the winner. No method is favoured in advance. The recommended forecast comes with a 50/80/95% range built from its own past errors, and the page shows how often that range held in the backtest. Pick one of nine published test series (the featured examples), any of the 100 Industry Market Watch series (grouped, with search), or upload your own CSV or Excel file. Everything runs in the browser; uploaded files never leave it.

## Page layout (Nov 2026 readability pass)
- **Answer first:** one line with the forecast for the chosen horizon and its 80% range, then three tiles (forecast, range and how often it held, error vs naive). If past ranges held less than 70% of the time, the line says so instead of "8 times in 10". The step-by-step table is folded under it.
- **Chart:** dots in the backtest window are the forecasts made one step before each date (every date in the window has one); a switch shows the forecasts made h steps before, which is what the table scores. For the pool they are re-picked at each origin exactly as in the backtest. A note appears when the recommended forecast's latest one-step misses are bigger than any earlier one in the window, because every method lags a sudden move and a range built from calmer errors understates the risk (Barber et al., 2023).
- **Why this method:** the reasons, the range check and the "testing the choice" table are in one collapsed section, one line each.
- **Table:** average miss (MAE) and change vs naive by default; MASE, RMSE, MSE, MAPE, sMAPE, bias, p-values and fitted parameters under "More measures". Ranking is unchanged (MASE, which orders methods as MAE does).

**Fitting.**
- Holt-Winters: α, β, γ and the starting level, trend and m seasonal terms are fitted together by minimising one-step squared error with a Nelder–Mead search (Nelder & Mead, 1965). This is the least-squares version of the ETS(A,A,A) fit in FPP3.
- Seasonal ARIMA: θ and Θ fitted by conditional sum of squares (Nelder–Mead).
- SES / Holt / damped: α, β ∈ [0.0001, 0.9999], φ ∈ [0.80, 0.98] and the starting level and trend fitted together by least squares (the `forecast::ets` bounds; Hyndman & Khandakar, 2008), with Nelder–Mead started from the best point of the old grid and restarted until it stops improving. `test/test_statsmodels.py` checks the fit against statsmodels with the same bounds (in-sample SSE within 1%; on the repo series the worst ratio is 1.0003).
- Holt-Winters keeps its Phase 1 fit: the audit's normalised-seasonals-plus-restarts version fitted closer in sample but forecast 1–2% worse at 6 and 12 months (below).

**Why these choices (tested before building).** On the five series (24-month window, 6 steps ahead), fitting Holt-Winters' starting states lowered its average MASE from 0.581 to 0.498, and seasonal ARIMA was the best method on both real seasonal series. Accuracy matches the statsmodels maximum-likelihood fits closely (Cass freight: Holt-Winters 0.408 here vs 0.404; seasonal ARIMA 0.393 vs 0.404). A nested test (choose the method on an older 24-month window, score it on the newest 12 months) beat the naive forecast on every series, which supports letting the backtest choose.

## Machine learning: tested before adding
`research-ml/` benchmarks every method on 80 real FRED series (Market Watch) plus 6 series with other time units (rolling origin, 6 steps ahead). Theta, ridge and seasonal ARIMA had the lowest median MASE (0.31). Picking the method on an older window and scoring it on the newest 12 months, the extra methods cut mean MASE on the real series only from 0.490 to 0.486 (Wilcoxon p = 0.42, better on 9, worse on 15). A 5-network MLP ranked worst and was ~6x slower, so it was left out. This matches Makridakis, Spiliotis & Assimakopoulos (2018) for short single series; ML won M5 with thousands of related series and covariates (Makridakis et al., 2022). The ML methods stay as candidates because they win on some series (e.g. ridge on exponential growth).

## Pool: one vote per distinct forecast, and its own table row (Nov 26 2026)
- **Duplicates.** SES with alpha at its 0.9999 bound is the naive method (Hyndman, Koehler, Ord & Snyder, 2008), and drift with almost no slope can match naive or Holt. The pool used to average such pairs as two members, doubling one forecast's weight. Now, walking the ranking, a method is skipped when its forecasts over the ranking window differ from a member's by at most 1% of that member's summed absolute error (`DUP_TOL`, `topDistinct` in engine.js); the next method takes its place, and the table marks it "same forecasts as …". Screening out near-copies before combining follows Lichtendahl & Winkler (2020).
- **Rule written before the test** (`pool-dedup/RULE.md` in the project files): keep it only if the re-picked pool's error (geometric mean new/old) is ≤ 1.005 at every horizon on the 80 Market Watch series (h = 1, 6, 12) and on the nine test series (h = 1, 6). Result: Market Watch 0.9997 / 0.9982 / 0.9961 (the pool changed at 428 of 1,920 one-step origins), test series 0.9985 / 1.0000; 80% range coverage unchanged to three decimals. Kept.
- **Pool row.** The ranked table now has a "Top-3 pool" row with every measure, placed where its score falls (`placeAmong`). It is the re-picked pool (members chosen at each past origin from the misses known then), so unlike the method rows its score is not flattered by the choice; when the history is too short to re-pick, the row is today's three and says it is flattered. Its p vs naive is the one under "Testing the choice" (Holm over pool and single pick); its p vs the best method is a single Diebold-Mariano test.

## Evaluation
- **Rolling origin** (Tashman, 2000; FPP3 §5.10): the backtest window is the last N values (12/24/36). Every method is re-fitted at each origin on data before it and forecasts 1..h steps ahead (h = 1/3/6/12). Only complete origins are scored (origins n−N to n−h, each with all h steps inside the data), so every step ahead is scored the same number of times; the window must be at least 2h. MAE by horizon is also shown.
- **Metrics:** MAE, MSE, RMSE, MAPE, sMAPE, MASE, Bias (mean error = actual − forecast). MAPE is reported as n/a if any actual is zero.
- **MASE scale** (Hyndman & Koehler, 2006): in-sample MAE of the seasonal-naive forecast (naive if seasonality = none) on the training data before the first origin. Note that multi-step errors are compared with a one-step scale, so MASE > 1 is normal at longer horizons. If that scale is zero (a flat history, such as a fixed contract quantity), MASE is undefined, so the page falls back to the naive MAE and then to the average level, and says so; rankings within a series do not change. Values filled into gaps are never scored.
- **Ranking:** lowest MASE (RMSE breaks ties). Within one series MASE orders methods exactly as MAE does; it is used because it is comparable across series.
- **Intermittent demand (Phase 5):** `demandPattern()` computes ADI (periods per period with demand) and CV² of the non-zero sizes and classifies the series as smooth, erratic, intermittent or lumpy (Syntetos, Boylan & Croston, 2005; cut-offs 1.32 and 0.49). A series that is never negative, has at least two non-zero values and ADI > 1.32 is ranked by RMSE (MASE breaks ties), the re-picked pool and pick are chosen on squared error, the Diebold–Mariano losses are per-origin mean squared scaled errors, and every forecast is floored at zero. MAE rewards forecasting zero on such series (Teunter & Duncan, 2009; Kolassa, 2016); stock and budgets need the mean, which squared error targets. Every other series is handled exactly as before.
- **Testing the choice (nested, inside each origin):** the table's #1 is flattered, because its score is the one it was picked on (Cawley & Talbot, 2010). So at every scored origin T the engine ranks all methods on the errors already known at T (the N values before T, complete origins only, exactly as the table ranks them at the end of the data) and forecasts with the top method ("best single method, re-picked") and with the equal-weight average of the top three ("top-3 pool"; Kourentzes, Barrow & Petropoulos, 2019). Both are scored on the same targets as the methods. This needs 2N fitted origins, so the page fits them as needed (`ensure()`); a series shorter than minTrain + 2N gets the table only, with a note.
- **Recommendation rule (fixed in advance):** the top-3 pool; the single best method only if, re-picked, it beat the pool with Diebold–Mariano p < 0.05; naive if the chosen approach did not beat naive in the re-picked test. KPIs show the recommended forecast's MAE relative to naive and to seasonal naive on the same forecasts (Davydenko & Fildes, 2013), because MASE < 1 against an in-sample seasonal-naive scale is nearly automatic for trending prices.
- **Significance:** Diebold–Mariano test (Diebold & Mariano, 1995) on per-origin mean absolute scaled errors, with an equal-weighted cosine (EWC) long-run variance, B = ⌊0.4·T^(2/3)⌋ and a Student-t(B) reference (fixed-b; Lazarus, Lewis, Stock & Watson, 2018; Coroneo & Iacone, 2020). The table's "p vs naive" and "vs #1" columns and the two re-picked approaches are each Holm-adjusted as a family (Holm, 1979). In the audit's simulation this kept false alarms at 4–6% where the old HLN version reached 15% (and 67% for "at least one row significant"). Power is low with 7–35 overlapping origins, so the page says "too short to tell" rather than "could be luck".
- **Forecast ranges:** the range around the recommended forecast comes from that approach's own past k-step errors (empirical intervals; Williams & Goodman, 1971; Lee & Scholtes, 2014), one set per step ahead k. It uses the newest 48 errors for each k and the split-conformal order statistics (Lei, G'Sell, Rinaldo, Tibshirani & Wasserman, 2018): the 80% range runs from the ⌊0.1(n+1)⌋-th to the ⌈0.9(n+1)⌉-th smallest error. A level needs enough errors for those ranks (39 for 95%); with fewer than 10 errors the page shows no range and says how long a window would give one. The median error gives the P50. A series that never goes below zero (prices, demand) gets ranges floored at zero; every actual is then at least zero, so this changes no coverage and only removes impossible values. **Check:** at every complete origin in the backtest window the range is rebuilt from the errors known at that origin only (targets at or before it) and scored on what happened, so the coverage shown is out of sample. The page also shows how often actuals fell above and below the range. With fewer than 20 checked forecasts it gives the count only and says that is too few to judge.
- **Speed:** every method is fitted once per origin (12 steps ahead); each horizon/window setting is then scored from those forecasts. A k-step forecast doesn't depend on how many steps were requested, so this is exact. Fitting runs in a Web Worker (`worker.js`, built from the same engine source), so the page stays responsive: the table and recommendation appear first, then the older origins the ranges need are fitted in the background with a progress note. Changing the series, horizon or window while it runs cancels the stale job. If the browser blocks workers, the same code runs on the page.

## Uploading your own data
Upload a CSV/TSV/text export or an Excel workbook (.xlsx). Everything is read in the browser (`upload.js`, no libraries; .xlsx is unzipped with the browser's built-in decompression).
- **Layout:** any number of columns and header rows. The value column is the one with the most numbers (you can switch if there are several); the date column is the one with the most readable dates. With no dates, rows are periods 1, 2, 3…
- **File quirks handled:** Excel BOM and UTF-16 "Unicode text", comma/semicolon/tab separators, Mac/Windows line endings, quoted cells, thousands separators, currency and % signs, (negatives), blank or text rows, newest-first order, duplicates, an .xlsx saved with a .csv name, data on a later sheet, and workbooks using Excel's 1904 date system. Old binary .xls gets a message to re-save as .xlsx or CSV; formulas whose results were never saved get a message to re-save in Excel.
- **Decimal mark:** judged from the number cells only (dates such as 01.02.2020 are ignored), so `1.234,5` and `1234,500` in a semicolon file read as 1234.5. When a file could go either way the page offers a Decimal mark toggle. A comma file with an unquoted number such as `1,210.5` (split into two cells) is joined back and flagged; one column of `10,5`-style numbers is read as decimals.
- **Gaps:** missing periods, blank values and NA cells inside a dated series are filled by a straight line between neighbours (the Market Watch rule) and listed on the page, so seasonal lags stay aligned. Filled values help fit the methods but are never scored. More than 10% missing is reported instead of filled. Dates that do not exist (30 February) are dropped with a warning, and two-digit years follow Excel (00–29 → 2000s, 30–99 → 1900s).
- **Dates:** ISO (2020-01-22), US and European numeric (1/22/2020, 22.01.2020), month names (22-Jan, Jan 22 2020, January 2020, Jan-22), quarters (2020Q1, Q1 2020), ISO weeks (2020-W05), years, times (1/22/2020 1:30 PM) and Excel date cells. When dates could be month/day or day/month, the page picks the reading that gives evenly spaced dates, says so, and offers a toggle.
- **Time unit:** detected from the spacing (seconds, minutes, hours, days, business days, weeks, months, quarters, years, or plain periods). Seasonality defaults to 60/60/24/7/5/52/12/4/none respectively (or none if there isn't enough history for two full cycles) and can be changed, including a custom length. Labels, horizon and backtest wording follow the unit.
- `test/uploads/` holds 31 test files (Excel-style CSVs, UTF-16, .xlsx, multi-sheet, quarterly, weekly, hourly, business-day, ambiguous dates, plus one file for each upload bug fixed in the Nov 2026 audit). `node test/test_uploads.js` loads each into the page in headless Chromium and checks count, first and last date, values, time unit, filled periods and messages against `test/expected_uploads.json`, and that the page renders with a finite MASE; it exits 1 on any mismatch. `node test/test_engine.js` checks engine edge cases (flat histories, non-numbers, filled values not scored, NaN-safe ranking, MASE unchanged by units) and the Phase 2 logic: complete origins, no look-ahead in the re-picked pool, pool = average of the methods picked at that origin, a 4,000-run Monte Carlo of the Diebold–Mariano false-alarm rate, Holm, lazy fitting, and the short-history fallback; and the Phase 3 ranges: chunked fitting matches one-pass fitting, conformal ranks for 48 and 19 errors, no look-ahead in the range check, coverage equals the share of actuals inside, short histories never throw, a 400-run random walk where the naive 80% range covers 78–86%, and the zero floor (coverage identical to the same series shifted below zero). The upload test also waits for the ranges and checks the 80% range figure.

## Verification
`verify.py` is an independent Python re-implementation (regression via numpy least squares, a different solver from the JS; the Diebold–Mariano p-values via scipy). `node dump_js.js` runs `engine.js` over 6 series (including the intermittent spare-parts sample) × 2 seasonality settings × 3 windows × 4 horizons (every window of at least 2h) plus daily, business-day, hourly, weekly and quarterly test series, plus the nine published test series at their own seasonality (window 24, h = 1 and 6; the weekly gasoline and daily Walmart series only with `FC_FULL=1`, since they take several minutes in Python), 160 cases with `FC_FULL=1`; `python3 verify.py` re-fits every method at every origin and compares every metric, MAE-by-horizon value, per-origin loss, 12-step future forecast and fitted parameter, the ranking, every EWC Diebold–Mariano p-value and its Holm adjustment, the re-picked top-3 pool and single pick (their members at every origin, metrics, losses and forecasts), and the forecast ranges for the pool, the single pick and naive (every future and check-row bound, P50, error count, zero floor, origins checked and coverage): **479,764 values (Nov 26 2026, `FC_FULL=1`; includes the same-forecast pairs, 14 of 160 cases, and the pool row place and p vs #1), worst relative difference 9.0e-10, rankings and picks identical; a NaN on either side counts as a mismatch** (Holm-adjusted p-values are allowed 1e-9 × the family size, because the adjustment multiplies a raw p-value by up to that factor). The Python check uses plain left-to-right sums because Python 3.12+ `sum()` is compensated and would make the optimiser drift from JavaScript's.

## Files
- `index.html`: the finished, self-contained page (GitHub Pages ready)
- `template.html`, `engine.js` (methods, backtest, metrics), `upload.js` (CSV/Excel reader, dates, time units), `mw.js` (loads and cleans Market Watch series), `worker.js` (runs the engine's fitting in a Web Worker), `app.js` (UI, SVG charts), `build.py` (inlines everything into index.html and builds the series catalog from a clone of the market-watch repo; set `MW_REPO`, default `../../market-watch`)
- `make_sample.py`, `data/*.csv`
- `dump_js.js`, `verify.py`: cross-check of the engine
- `test/dump_mw.js`, `test/verify_mw.py`: cross-check of the Market Watch loader (all 100 series, 24,432 values identical to an independent Python/numpy clean-up; the engine runs on every series with finite metrics)
- `research/`: the experiments behind the method choices (`compare_fit.py` vs statsmodels, `proto.py` + `score.py`)
- `preview/`: screenshots

Rebuild: `python3 build.py`. Verify: `node dump_js.js && python3 verify.py`, and `node test/dump_mw.js ../../market-watch > /tmp/mw.json && python3 test/verify_mw.py ../../market-watch /tmp/mw.json`.
